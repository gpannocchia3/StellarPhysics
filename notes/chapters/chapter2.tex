\chapter{Stellar Structures}\label{ch:ii}

\section{Stellar Equilibrium Equations}

In this chapter we're going to introduce and describe the so-called \emph{stellar equilibrium equations}, which account for the physical mechanisms that are interplaying within stellar structures, and compare it with what real-life observations tell us.
\par The goal of stellar structure equations is making possible to predict instant after instant in every point of the star the value of each physical and chemical properties, so that once an initial condition is set, we're going to be virtually able to accurately determine the evoluition of those properties.
\par On a first approximation, we can consider stars to be autogravitating mass conglomerates, for which the gravitational pull is evenly balanced by the pressure gradients (and possibly other forces) that set in within the star. Since gravity is a \emph{central force}, we can well expect the equilibrium configuration to be that of a sphere. This greatly simplifies the problem at hand, since from a three-dimensional configuration is reduced to a one-dimensional configuration by means of symmetry.
\par The approximation of spherical symmetry allows us to formally decompose the star in a series of \emph{shells} of infinitesimal width $\text{d}r$, so that the physical properties of the star are approximately constant withing said shell. The quantities we'll be interested in considering are the pressure $p(r)$, the density $\rho(r)$, the temperature $T(r)$, the various relative abundances $X_{i}(r)$, the mass $M(r)$ (that is considered to be the mass contained in the sphere of radius $r$) and the luminosity $L(r)$ (the energy per unit time entering the shell at distance $r$ from the center of the star).
\par Given this notation, the mass found inside radius $r+\text{d}r$ should be $M(r)+\text{d}M$, where $\text{d}M$ can be identified with the mass of the infinitesimal spherical shell. Said $\rho(r)$ the density, then the mass of this shell is
\begin{equation*}
	\text{d}M = 4\pi r^{2}\rho\,\text{d}r
\end{equation*}
which yields our first stellar structure equation
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
		\frac{\text{d}M}{\text{d}r} = 4\pi r^{2}\rho(r)
	}
	\label{eq:massconservation}
\end{equation}
Note that (\ref{eq:massconservation}) is essentially a restating of the continuity equation of mass.
\par Let's stop for a moment to consider how sensible are the two approximations we're implementing in our description, that are the request of spherical symmetry and that only pressure gradients are balancing the gravitational pull. The former is generally met by the vast majority of stars, while for the latter it might be necessary to use some more care, especially when dealing with (highly) rotating stars, forcing us to add centrifugal forces to the picture\footnote{Note that in the presence of rotation, the equilibrium configuration is no longer spherically symmetric, since rather than the gravitational potential, we have now to consider a baroclyinic potential (gravitational+centrifugal).}.
\par Generally, if the centrifugal acceleration at the surface is too high with respect to the surface gravitational acceleration, the necessity of modifying our equations to keep track of centrifugal forces may arise.
\par For this purpose, we define the \emph{critical angular velocity} $\Omega_{c}$ so that
\begin{equation}
	\Omega_{c}^{2} \sim \frac{GM_{*}}{R_{*}^{3}} \implies v_{c} \sim \sqrt{\frac{GM_{*}}{R_{*}}}
	\label{eq:critical_av}
\end{equation}
Note that depending on the mass and the radius of the star we're considering, critical velocities might get as high as $v_{c} \sim 300-600\,\text{km}/\text{s}$; for comparison, the equatorial velocity of the Sun is $v_{\odot}^{\text{eq}} \sim 2\, \text{km}/\text{s}$.
\par To result in non-negligible effects, it is sufficient that the angular velocity gets as high as $\Omega_{\text{sup}} \gtrsim 0.5\Omega_{c}$; if, instead, $\Omega_{\text{sup}} \gtrsim 0.7\Omega_{c}$ you start observing deformations in the structure of the star, but this are fairly rare. As you may easily guess, these are problems that concern with the more massive stars, getting more and more relevant as the mass of the star gets bigger.
\par About $20\%$ of the stars with mass in the range $10M_{\odot}\lesssim M \lesssim 19M_{\odot}$ (so typically stars of spectral class B or O) have velocities of order
\begin{equation*}
	150 < v_{\sin i} < 300-400\,\text{km}/\text{s}
\end{equation*}
Note that through Doppler effect, we're able to measure the velocity of objects along the line of sight, but in principle, the rotation axis of the object we're observing may be tilted of an angle $i$ with respect to the observer, hence the notation/factor $\sin i$.
\par Increasing the mass, we find that about $30\%$ of the stars with mass in the range $20M_{\odot}\lesssim M \lesssim 40M_{\odot}$ have roughly the rotational velocities of the order of the ones we've written above.
\par To "detect" the presence and relevance of rotation it is customary to perform a spectroscopic analysis of the star. In fact, it is clear that the presence of rotation issues the arising of convection motion within the star, which in turn implies a "mixing" of the star's contents.
\par In principle, if we observe main sequence stars, the chemical composition of the surface should be roughly equal to the starting composition\footnote{If we neglect the effects of \emph{sedimentation}, that is. The characteristic times are however too short for sedimentation to occur, so it should be probably safe neglecting it.}, for temperature isn't high enough to start the nuclear processing on the surface. But, in presence of strong mixing mechanisms, we may observe an excess of Helium and Nitrogen that are brought to the surface from the inner regions of the star (where nuclear reactions are at least partially active).

\subsection*{The Effect of Magnetic Fields (anticipation)}
\par In principle, even particularly strong magnetic fields may influence the surface composition and structure of the star. This, however, requires
\begin{equation*}
	\beta = \frac{p_{\text{gas}}}{p_{\text{mag}}} \sim 1 \; \text{with} \; \beta = \frac{8\pi p}{B^{2}}
\end{equation*}
that in turn would mean having magnetic fields as high as $B \approx 3000-5000\,\text{Gauss}$. In general they are not relevant, but there exist very rare cases such that of \emph{magnetic stars} of class $A_{p}$ or $B_{p}$, where the subscript "p" is to indicate particular lines in the spectrum of such stars. For comparison
\begin{equation*}
	\langle B \rangle _{\odot} \sim 0.5-4\,\text{Gauss} \; \text{vs} \; \langle B \rangle _{MS} \sim 1-30\,\text{kGauss}
\end{equation*}
\par Magnetic fields that strong are assured to influence the external layers of such stars, inducing mixings that lead to a modification of the surface abundances.
\par Another class of magnetic stars is that of \emph{magnetic white dwarves} that, although very rare, can reach magnetic fields of order $10^{6}-10^{9}\,\text{Gauss}$. Similarly, in \emph{magnetars} (magnetic neutron stars), magnetic fields can reach intensities of $10^{14}-10^{15}\,\text{Gauss}$.
\par Note that in principle we're able to detect the presence of strong magnetic fields by studying the surface oscillation of the star, performing what's called \emph{asteroseismology}.

\subsection{Free Fall Time}

\par Our initial claim was that for the majority of their life, stars are in \emph{hydrostatic equilibrium}, in a configuration where pressure gradients are evenly matching the collapse forces. But can we be so sure that a star is, as a matter of fact, in equilibrium?
\par We know, for example, that the Sun has been stable on timescales of at least a couple hundred years. On top of that, looking at the Milky way we can find billions of stars with characteristics similar to those of the Sun but in different stages of their life, so that we might have an idea of the whole life cycle of our dearest yellow dwarf.
\par From those observations, we learn that the Sun must have been stable for times of the order of millions of years. Of course, to make sure it is indeed in hydrostatic equilibrium, we should compare with the \emph{dynamical timescales} the observation timescales over which we can safely claim that its characteristics (e.g., the luminosity and the radius) haven't changed.
\par Let's consider a spherically symmetric star with constant density. In presence of gravity alone, we can write 
\begin{equation*}
	\ddot{r} = -\frac{GM}{r^{2}}
\end{equation*}
which can be immediately integrated to yield 
\begin{align*}
    \frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2} &= \frac{GM}{r}+\text{const.}\\
    &= \frac{GM}{r}-\frac{GM}{R}
\end{align*}
where we have set the initial conditions for $t=0$: $r=R$ and $\dot{r} = 0$.
\par The free-fall timescale can be thus obtained by committing a mathematical atrocity, but I (and, for extension, you too) don't care\footnote{It is actually less atrocious than I'm making it sound like.}
\begin{align*}
    \tau_{ff} &= \int_{0}^{t_{ff}} \text{d}t = \int_{R}^{0}\frac{1}{\dot{r}}\,\text{d}r \\
    &= \int_{0}^{R} \frac{\text{d}r}{\sqrt{2GM\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{R}\right)}} = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{R^{3}}{GM}}
\end{align*}
thus, if we use $R^{3}/GM = 3/4\pi G\rho$
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{ff} = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32G\rho}} \approx 1.63\,\text{Myr}\cdot\left(\frac{10^{3}\,\text{cm}^{-3}}{\mu n}\right)^{1/2}
    \label{eq:ff_timescale}
\end{equation}
\par For the Sun, the free-fall timescale amounts to roughly $\tau_{ff} \sim 30\,\text{min}-1\,\text{h}$ depending on the calculation that has been used. So we can safely claim that the Sun is definitely not collapsing. What if, instead, it is exploding?\footnote{Read: expanding outwards.}
\par To see that we can calculate the explosion time, sound time, or dynamical time as 
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\text{sound}} = \frac{R}{c_{s}} \approx 0.5\,\text{Myr}\,\left(\frac{R}{0.1\,\text{pc}}\right)\left(\frac{0.2\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{c_{s}}\right)
    \label{eq:sound_time}
\end{equation}
If $\tau_{ff}>\tau_{\text{sound}}$ the system returns to a stable equilibrium as the pressure forces can overcome gravity. Conversely, for $\tau_{ff}<\tau_{\text{sound}}$ gravitational collapse takes place.

\subsection{Hydrostatic Equilibrium}

In the last section we showed that is fair for us to assume that stars are in \emph{hydrostatic equilibrium} for most of their lives. We now turn to write an equation (the second of four) that will allow to characterize our star.
\par Consider a star in which the only relevant forces acting on a spherical shell between $r$ and $r+\text{d}r$ and transverse area $\text{d}A$ are the gravitational pull and the pressure forces (both due to the gas and photons). It's not difficult to show that the balance condition for the mass of the small element is
\begin{equation*}
	-\text{d}p\text{d}A -\frac{GM_{r}}{r^{2}}\rho\text{d}r\text{d}A = 0
\end{equation*}
from which 
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
		\frac{\text{d}P}{\text{d}r} = -\frac{GM_{r}}{r^{2}}
	}
	\label{eq:mechanical_eq}
\end{equation}
A quick look at (\ref{eq:massconservation}) and (\ref{eq:mechanical_eq}) will convince you that we have more free variables (functions of the radial coordinate) than equations. This is one of the many cases in which the \emph{closure problem} pops out. To solve it we're gonna need to introduce an equation of state (EoS)
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
		p = p(\rho, T, X, Y, X_{i})
	}
	\label{eq:general_eos}
\end{equation}
where the various quantities have their obvious meaning. A particularly simple configuration (that we'll have the pleasure to look somewhere in the following) is the one where we're allowed to take a \emph{politropic equation of state}
\begin{equation}
	p = K\rho^{\gamma}
	\label{eq:politropic_eos}
\end{equation}
with $K$ some constant and $\gamma$ some power that depends on the particular gas and its degenerate state.
\par Let us now take a look at another particularly simple EoS, that is the equation of state of perfect (non degenerate) gas
\begin{equation}
	p = nkT = \frac{\rho(r)}{\mu(r) m_{H}}kT(r)
	\label{eq:perfectgas_eos}
\end{equation}
with $\mu(r)$ the \emph{mean molecular weight}, which clearly depends on the elements present in the star and their ionization state, which can (hopefully) inferred from considering Saha's equation (\ref{eq:saha}). In general it's not easy to determine the exact form of $\mu(r)$ so it is customary to consider the easy scenario of a \emph{completely ionized} star\footnote{People tend to say that a star is completely ionized when its temperature is at least $T > 10^{6\,\text{K}}$, although that's a completely arbitrary lower limit. For instance, heavier elements like Iron, which have electrons to spare, are completely ionized at much higher temperatures.}
\par If we consier $X$ the relative abundance of Hydrogen, $Y$ the relative abundance of Helium and $X_{i}$ the relative abundance of the metal with $i$ so that $A>4$\footnote{Sorry $^{4}_{3}\text{He}$, you're too less abundant to be considered. But your time will come soon.}. If we consider the number of ions and electrons that complete ionization generates:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Hydrogen: \#ions $X/m_{H}$, \#electrons $X/m_{H}$;
	\item Helium: \#ions $Y/4m_{H}$, \#electrons $2Y/4m_{H}$;
	\item Metal $i$-th: \#ions $X_{i}/A_{i}m_{H}$, \#electrons $Z_{i}X_{i}/A_{i}m_{H}$
\end{itemize}
so that the total number of particles (per unit volume) is just
\begin{equation}
	n = \left[2X+\frac{3Y}{4}+\sum_{i=4}^{N}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{A_{i}}+\frac{Z_{i}X_{i}}{A_{i}}\right)\right]\frac{\rho}{m_{H}}
	\label{eq:completeionization_species}
\end{equation}
\par If we consider the following approximation for (\ref{eq:completeionization_species})
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{X_{i}}{A_{i}}\ll 1 \quad \frac{Z_{i}}{A_{i}}\sim \frac{1}{2}
\end{equation*}
we find
\begin{equation}
	\mu^{-1} \approx 2X + \frac{3Y}{4} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=4}^{N} X_{i} = 2X + \frac{3Y}{4} + \frac{Z}{2}
	\label{eq:molecularweight}
\end{equation}
where we've introduced the \emph{metallicity} $Z$.
\par We can also consider the molecular weight restricted to ions only
\begin{equation*}
	\mu_{i}^{-1} = 2X + \frac{3Y}{4} +\sum_{i = 4}^{N}\frac{X_{i}}{A_{i}}
\end{equation*}
and consider an average charge for the mixture of elements $\bar{Z}$, so that $n_{e} = \bar{Z}n_{i} \approx \bar{Z}\rho/\mu_{i}m_{H}$. From this we can define the mean molecular weight of electrons as 
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\mu_{i}}{\bar{Z}} \to n_{e} = \frac{\rho}{\mu_{e}m_{H}}
\end{equation*}
\par If we plug this all back in the EoS for the perfect gas 
\begin{equation*}
	p_{\text{gas}} = nkT = (n_{e}+n_{i})kT = \frac{\rho k T}{m_{h}}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}+\frac{1}{\mu_{e}}\right)
\end{equation*}
we conclude
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{\mu_{i}}+\frac{1}{\mu_{e}}
	\label{eq:mwcomposition}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Temperature Gradients}

\par As you probably know, in the inner regions of stars holds the so called \emph{Local Thermodynamic Equilibrium} (for friends, LTE): In these regions there is, as the name suggests, punctual and \emph{local} thermodynamic equilibrium between matter, which will be then well described by a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, and radiation, which can be described by (\ref{eq:blackbodyintensity}).
\par This fact should also suggest you that, since we're able to observe temperature gradients within stars, it is not possible for stars to be in \emph{stric thermodynamic equilibrium} (STE) and thus emit like perfect blackbodies with fixed temperature.
\par Let us try to give an order of magnitude estimate of the temperature gradient within, say, the Sun
\begin{equation*}
	\langle\,\frac{\text{d}T}{\text{d}r}\,\rangle = \frac{T_{c}-T_{S}}{R_{\odot}} \approx 10^{-4}\,\text{K}\,\text{cm}^{-1}
\end{equation*}
which means that over the average mean free path of a photon in the inner regions of stars ($\lambda\sim 0.1-1\,\text{cm}$), the difference in temperature will be
\begin{equation*}
	\Delta T = \langle\,\frac{\text{d}T}{\text{d}r}\,\rangle\cdot \lambda \approx 10^{-5}-10^{-4}\,\text{K}
\end{equation*}
which is very small. But to actually check if the assumption of LTE is valid (if at all sensible) we shall consider the variation in internal energy of the photons. We know that in thermal equilibrium $U = aT^{4}$, so 
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\text{d}U}{U} = 4\frac{\text{d}T}{T} \sim 10^{-12}-10^{-11}
\end{equation*}
which means the deviation from isotropic (blackbody) behavior is negligibly small over the mean free path of photons, therefore allowing us to assume the LTE configuration. 
\par We know, either through direct observation or because you've read the upcoming section about radiative transfer, that wherever is a temperature gradient there is also a radiative flux (along with a convective motion and electronic conduction)!
\par On a first approximation we may write
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\text{d}T(r)}{\text{d}r}\Big|_{\text{rad}} \propto F(r)\bar{\kappa}
\end{equation*}
wnere $F$ is the flux we've defined in (\ref{eq:bolometricflux}) and $\bar{\kappa}$ is some average \emph{opacity}\footnote{Opacity is measured in $\text{cm}^{2}\,\text{g}^{-1}$.} that sums up the various interaction channels between matter and radiation. Note that in general it is frequency dependent\footnote{For a more rigorous definition see either the next chapter or \cite{1986rpa..book.....R}.}.
\par In the equation we've written above we're assuming that the transport of energy is carried out by radiation alone, which is essentially the temperature gradient that we'd observe if all the flux was transported by radiation.
\par Taking a closer look to the micro-physics involved in absorption phenomena we learn that is possible to link the opacity to the mean free path of electrons
\begin{equation*}
	\lambda_{\sigma} = (n\langle\sigma\rangle)^{-1} = (\rho\bar{\kappa})^{-1}
\end{equation*}
\par If, on the other hand, there were another channel available for transporting energy on top of radiation, we may be writing
\begin{equation*}
	F(r) = \frac{L(r)}{4\pi r^{2}} = F_{\text{rad}}+F_{\text{conv}}
\end{equation*}
where we've assumed the presence of, say, relevant convective motions within the star. It should go without saying that the temperature gradient will no longer be the gradient induced by radiative transport, but will rather be an \emph{ambient gradient}, which we might express as 
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\text{d}T(r)}{\text{d}r}\Big|_{\text{amb}} \propto \bar{\kappa}F_{\text{rad}}
\end{equation*}
Clearly, since now radiation is transporting only a part of the total flux
\begin{equation*}
	\Big|\frac{\text{d}T(r)}{\text{d}r}\Big|_{\text{amb}} < \Big|\frac{\text{d}T(r)}{\text{d}r}\Big|_{\text{rad}}
\end{equation*}
\par If we define with $\phi$ the flux of photons per unit time, and approximate the number of interactions in an infinitesimal distance $\text{d}r$ as $\text{d}r/\lambda$, with $\lambda$ the mean free path, we can calculate the transferred momentum
\begin{equation*}
	\delta p = \phi \frac{\text{d}r}{\lambda}\frac{h\nu}{c} = \frac{F(r)}{\lambda c}\text{d}r
\end{equation*}
But the variation in (linear) momentum is equal to the variation of pressure $\delta p = \delta P_{\text{rad}}$
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\text{d}P_{\text{rad}}}{\text{d}r} = \frac{F(r)}{\lambda c}
\end{equation*}
If we recall that $P_{\text{rad}} = U/3 = aT^{4}/3$ and correct the sign of the expression (to address the mismatch of signs between the flux and temperature gradient), we finally yield
\begin{equation*}
	F(r) = -\lambda c a \frac{4}{3}T^{3}\frac{\text{d}T}{\text{d}r}
\end{equation*}
Recalling that $\lambda = (\rho\bar{\kappa})^{-1}$ and that $\sigma_{B} = ac/4$ we can write our third stellar structure equation
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
		F(r) = -\frac{16}{3}\frac{\sigma_{B}T^{3}}{\rho\bar{\kappa}}\frac{\text{d}T}{\text{d}r}
	}
	\label{eq:energytransport}
\end{equation}
It is often useful to flip the equation
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\text{d}T(r)}{\text{d}r}\Big|_{\text{rad}} = -\frac{3\bar{\kappa}\rho}{4ac}\frac{1}{T^{3}}\frac{L(r)}{4\pi r^{2}}
\end{equation*}
so that in general, if $F_{\text{rad}}\neq F_{\text{tot}}$, we can make a slight manipulation and write
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\text{d}T(r)}{\text{d}r}\Big|_{\text{amb}} = -\frac{3\bar{\kappa}\rho}{4ac}\frac{1}{T^{3}}F_{\text{rad}}
\end{equation*}
If you want to see a slight more rigorous derivation of (\ref{eq:energytransport}), you may check the last part of the "Radiative Transfer" section.

\subsection{Energy Production}

Let us consider what happens if the stellar atmosphere was not constantly replenished with energy from the inner layers. How long would it take for the star to deplete completely the energy stored in the atmosphere.
\par The average density of the outer layers of the atmosphere is $\rho^{\text{ext}_{\odot}}\sim 10^{-7}\,\text{g}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$. We can thus give an estimate of the mean free path $\lambda_{j}\sim (\rho\bar{\kappa})^{-1}\sim 100-300\,\text{km}$. We've made the (usually decent) approximation of opacity being dominated by photoelectric effect on $\text{H}^{-}$ ions, $\bar{\kappa} \approx 0.3\,\text{cm}^{2}\,\text{g}^{-1}$. Note that in these conditions, the width of the atmosphere is of the same order of the mean free path of photons.
\par We know the solar flux $F_{\odot} \sim 6 \cdot 10^{10}\,\text{erg}\,\text{cm}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, as well as its temperature and pressure $T_{eff}\sim 6000\,\text{K}$, $p\approx 10^{5}\,\text{dyne}\,\text{cm}^{-2}$. The average number of particles is $n\sim 10^{17}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, which can be integrated over the vertical direction to find an average number of photons per unit surface.
\par The mean energy of photons can be expressed as $E_{p} = 3kT/2\sim 10^{-12}\,\text{erg}$. The energy per unit surface is thus $E_{s}\approx 10^{12}\,\text{erg}\,\text{cm}^{-2}$. Finally, we find that the energy stored in the atmosphere would be depleted in roughly
\begin{equation*}
	\tau = \frac{E_{s}}{F} \approx 15\,\text{s}
\end{equation*}
In such a short time, we would be able to discern immediately the changes in color in the atmosphere. Similarly, through the random-walk approximation, we may find an estimate of the time it takes photons to escape the inner regions of stars.

\subsubsection*{Random Walks}

\par Consider a photon emitted in an infinite, homogeneous scattering region. It travels a displacement $\vec{r}_{1}$ before being scattered, then travels in a new direction over a displacement $\vec{r}_{2}$ before being scattered, and so on. After N free paths, the total displacement will look like 
\begin{equation*}
    \vec{R} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{r}_{i}
\end{equation*}
The average total displacement will be identically null. Therefore, we must evaluate the mean square of the total displacement, which, in the assumption of independent and isotropic scattering, must be expressed as 
\begin{equation*}
    l_{*}^{2} = \langle\vec{R}^{2}\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\vec{r}_{i}^{\,2}\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{N} l^{2}_{i}
\end{equation*}
The quantity $l_{*}$ is the root-mean-square net displacement of the photon, while $l^{2}$ denotes the mean square of the free path of a photon, which within a factor of order unity, it's simply the mean free path of a photon.
\par Since the mean square displacements of each scattering iteration have no reason to be different, we'll just write 
\begin{equation*}
    l_{*} = \sqrt{N}l
\end{equation*}
Said $L$ the linear dimension of the object photons are trying to "escape" from, the number of scatterings events required to actually escape from regions of large optical depth is roughly determined by setting $l_{*} \approx L$. Then $N \sim L^{2}/l^{2}$.
\par This way, we can find the number of interactions needed to travel through a solar radius $R_{\odot}$ if the average displacement is a mean free path $\lambda$
\begin{equation*}
	\Delta x = \sqrt{z}\lambda \implies z\approx\left(\frac{R_{\odot}}{\lambda}\right)^{2} = 1.96\cdot10^{22}
\end{equation*}
If an interaction takes, on average, $\tau = 10^{-8}\,\text{s}$, the time needed to perform all those interactions is $\Delta t \approx 10\,\text{Myr}$. This usually goes by the name of \emph{thermal timescale}.
\par If we consider the average density of a star and a mean opacity independent of temperature and density, we can write
\begin{equation*}
	\bar{\rho} = \frac{M}{\frac{4\pi}{3}R^{3}}
\end{equation*}
but for stars with mass in the range $M_{\odot}\lesssim M \lesssim 10M_{\odot}$, mass scales linearly with the radius $M\propto R$. So
\begin{equation*}
	\lambda = (\bar{\kappa}\rho)^{-1} \propto R^{2} \to \sqrt{z} \propto R^{-1} \implies \Delta t_{th} \propto R^{-2}
\end{equation*}
This is telling us that increasing the mass of a star (and thus the radius) has the surprising consequence of a (very) reduced lifespan.
\newline 
\par We now present the last equation of stellar structure, that relates the luminosity gradient to the rate of production of energy
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
		\frac{\text{d}L(r)}{\text{d}r} = 4\pi r^{2} \rho(r)\epsilon(r)
	}
	\label{eq:energyproduction}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon(r)$ is the "energy production rate", that is usually decomposed in three contributions
\begin{equation*}
	\epsilon(r) = \epsilon_{g}(r) + \epsilon_{N}(r) - \epsilon_{\nu}(r)
\end{equation*}
The easier to address is the term $-\epsilon_{\nu}$, which expresses the energy lost to neutrinos that are produced where there's no nuclear reactions. These neutrinos, produced via various channels, are often called \emph{thermal neutrinos}. The main channels of production are summed up below:
\begin{align*}
	e^{-} + (Z, A)\;\text{or}\;e^{-} & \to e^{-} + (Z,A)\;\text{or}\;e^{-} +\nu_{e}+\bar{\nu}_{e} \quad \text{Bremsstrahlung}\\
	\gamma + e^{-} & \to e^{-} +\nu_{e}+\bar{\nu}_{e} \quad \text{Photoproduction}\\
	\gamma & \to e^{-} + e^{-} \to +\nu_{e}+\bar{\nu}_{e} \quad \text{Pair annihilation}\\
	\gamma_{\text{plasmon}} & \to \nu_{x} + \bar{\nu}_{x} \quad \text{Neutrinic oscillations} \\
	(Z,A) + e^{-} & \to (Z-1,A)+\nu_{e} \quad \text{Neutralization}
\end{align*}
\par Please note that the subject of neutrinic oscillations is rather technical and I know nothing of it to make this surely intriguing subject justice. The only thing I know is that the collective oscillations in a plasma, \emph{plasmons}\footnote{Not to be confused with the omonimous biscuits.}, may decay into one of the three leptonic pairs of neutrinos.
\par The next one to address is the contribution given by \emph{gravitational energy} $\epsilon_{g}$. Consider the 1st principle of thermodynamics
\begin{equation}
	\text{d}Q = \text{d}U + p\text{d}V \quad \text{d}Q_{m} = \text{d}U_{m} + p\text{d}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)
	\label{eq:1st_principle}
\end{equation}
where the subscript $m$ in the second equation means "per unit mass". We may then define $\epsilon_{g} = -\text{d}Q_{m}/\text{d}t$. Invoking entropy, this becomes
\begin{equation*}
	\epsilon_{g} = -\frac{\text{d}Q_{m}}{\text{d}t} = -T\frac{\text{d}S_{m}}{\text{d}t} = -T\left[\partial_{p}S)_{T}\frac{\text{d}p}{\text{d}t}+\partial_{T}S_{m})_{p}\frac{\text{d}T}{\text{d}t}\right]
\end{equation*}
which, once we introduce the \emph{specific heat capacity at constant pressure} $c_{p} = T\partial_{T}S_{m})_{p}$ and the heat exchanged during an isothermal transformation $E_{T} = -T\partial_{p}S)_{T}$, we may finally write
\begin{equation}
	\epsilon_{g} = -\frac{\text{d}Q_{m}}{\text{d}t} = E_{T}\dot{p}-c_{p}\dot{T}
	\label{eq:gravitationalenergy_productionrate}
\end{equation}
\par Since time derivatives are involved, it should be clear that we are in the need of having two different models to describe the structure of a star in different times. To do so, it is necessary to solve \emph{mesh by mesh} the stellar structure equations. It is then of crucial importance knowing how abundances change within a given mesh. Those who work with this stuff usually linearize the function describing the evolution of the abundance of the $i$-th element as such
\begin{equation}
	X_{i,k}^{\text{new}} = X_{i,k}^{\text{old}} + \frac{\text{d}X_{i}}{\text{d}t}\Delta t
	\label{eq:linearized_abundancevolution}
\end{equation}
where the subscript $k$ refers to a given mesh. The time interval $\Delta t$ is often chosen so that in each mesh the physical variables and/or the abundances do not vary more than a predetermined value.

\section{Virial Theorem}

For stellar structures in hydrostatic equilibrium, for which only pressure and gravitational forces are present, the following \emph{virial theorem} holds
\begin{equation}
	2\langle K \rangle_{\text{trasl}} + \langle \Omega \rangle = \frac{1}{2}\ddot{I} \approx 0
	\label{eq:virialtheorem}
\end{equation}
\par This strikingly simple equation allows us to find some enlightning relations. Let us then consider a monoatomic perfect gas for which the equaipartition theorem grants us $\langle K \rangle = 3kT/2 = E_{th}$. The virial theorem thus implies
\begin{equation*}
	E_{th} = -\frac{\Omega}{2} > 0 \implies E_{tot} = \frac{\Omega}{2}
\end{equation*}
\par Note that under the assumption of spherical symmetry, the gravitational energy can be found evaluating the following integral
\begin{equation*}
	\Omega = -\int_{0}^{R_{*}}\frac{GM_{r}}{r}4\pi\rho r^{2}\,\text{d}r < 0
\end{equation*}
\par From the former expression, we can calculate the luminosity
\begin{equation*}
	L = -\der{E_{tot}}{t} = -\frac{1}{2}\der{\Omega}{t} = \der{E_{th}}{t} > 0
\end{equation*}
From this we're learning that from the gravitational contraction, half the energy is dissipated through radiation, the other half is used to "fuel" the thermal content of the star (i.e., the star gets hotter).
\par What if we relax the condition of the gas being monoatomic? The virial theorem (\ref{eq:virialtheorem}) still holds, but in general we can't say $\langle K \rangle = E_{th}$. The traslational (average) kinetic energy is still $\langle K \rangle = 3kT/2$, but now $E_{th} = l kT/2$, where $l$ is the number of degrees of freedom of the single gas element. It isn't particularly convenient to work with the degrees of freedom, so we're going to convert it to a more thermodynamically acceptable quantity, the adiabatix index $\gamma$. It is possible to show that
\begin{equation*}
	\gamma = \frac{C_{p}}{C_{V}} = 1 + \frac{2}{l}
\end{equation*}
This way we can cast the virial theorem in an alternative form
\begin{equation*}
	3(\gamma -1)E_{th} + \Omega \approx 0
\end{equation*}
\par The total energy can be calculated with a direct substitution
\begin{equation*}
	E_{tot} = E_{th}+\Omega = \frac{3\gamma -4}{3(\gamma -1)}\Omega
\end{equation*}
from which
\begin{align*}
	\Delta E_{tot} &= \frac{3\gamma -4}{3(\gamma -1)}\Delta\Omega\\
	\Delta E_{th} &= -\frac{\Delta\Omega}{3(\gamma -1)}
\end{align*}
Since $\Delta\Omega$ is necessarily smaller than 0, we thus obtain a stability criterion for the star: A star can be stable if $\gamma > 4/3$. If such condition is not met, all the energy released by gravitational collapse is converted in thermal energy, thus resulting in instabilities.
\par We can also extend the virial theorem to different equations of state if we allow for the translational kinetic energy to be written as $\langle K \rangle = \xi E_{th}$. Clearly, we have to assume that the coefficient $\xi$ is constant over the whole stellar structure. Repeating the same calculations as before (\emph{mutatis mutandis}, that is), virial theorem yields
\begin{equation*}
	E_{tot} = \frac{\xi - 1}{\xi}\Omega
\end{equation*}
and the stability condition therefore becomes $\xi > 1$.

\subsection{Thermal Timescale Revisited}

A few pages ago we made use of the random walk approximation to find the time that it takes on average to a photon to scatter its way out of the inner region of a star. We called that time \emph{thermal timescale}. It is the aim of this section to show that it is indeed possible to find an equivalent formulation for that timescale starting from the virial theorem.
\par Let us recall (\ref{eq:virialtheorem})
\begin{equation*}
	2\langle K \rangle + \langle \Omega \rangle \approx 0
\end{equation*}
For the sake of simplicity, we're going to assume a monoatomic gas, so that $\langle K \rangle = E_{th}$. We can assume the following expression for the gravitational potential
\begin{equation*}
	\Omega = -q\frac{GM^{2}}{R}
\end{equation*}
with $q$ that depends on the specific density profile that is given. For a homogeneous star, for example, $q=3/5$. We showed that the luminosity of the star will be such that
\begin{equation*}
	L = -\frac{1}{2}\dot{\Omega} = -\frac{q}{2}\frac{GM^{2}}{R^{2}}\dot{R}
\end{equation*}
Separating variables and integrating, we find
\begin{equation}
	\tau_{KH} = \frac{q}{2}\frac{GM^{2}}{RL}
	\label{eq:kh_timescale}
\end{equation}
which is called \emph{Kelvin-Helmoltz timescale}. In short, it represents the time during which the stellar structure is able to sustain itself radiating away the energy generated from the gravitational shrinking.
\par Note that we're implicitly assuming that the luminosity doesn't change while the star collapses, which is in general a very bad approximation.
\par Under the approximation of homogeneous density $q = 3/5$, and the Kelvin-Helmoltz timescale is
\begin{equation*}
	\tau_{KH} \approx 10\,\text{Myr}\,\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{R_{\odot}}{R}\right)\left(\frac{L_{\odot}}{L}\right)
\end{equation*}
which is much greater than the other dynamical times, but still not quite as large as it should be to match the estimated lifespan of the Sun (that it's also connected to the fairly long half-life of some radionuclides we observe here on Earth).

\section{Qualitative Stellar Structure Relations}

Now that we have the virial theorem at our disposal, we migth think of looking for very approximated expressions that can give us an idea of how physical properties in a star are interconnected. All quantities will be interpreted as \emph{averaged} quantities.
\par Let us start with density and temperature. If we know $M$ and $R$, then
\begin{equation*}
	\Omega = -q\frac{GM^{2}}{R}
\end{equation*}
with $q$ a constant given by the profile density. We can now use the virial theorem and invert the relation between mass, density and radius
\begin{equation*}
	E_{th} \sim \frac{3kT}{2}\frac{M}{\mu m_{\text{H}}} \quad \rho = \frac{M}{\frac{4\pi}{3}R^{3}}
\end{equation*}
finding 
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
		T \propto M^{2/3}\rho^{1/3}
	}
	\label{eq:temperature_density}
\end{equation}
This means that two stars with different masses must have different densities to achieve the same temperature! This will then reflect onto smaller stars having the higher densities.
\par Upon a first approximation, the luminosity $L$ does not depend on the energy production rate $\epsilon$. If we butcher the equation of hydrostatic equilibrium a plug in the proportionalities of the density
\begin{equation*}
	p_{C} = \frac{M^{2}}{R^{4}}
\end{equation*}
where $p_{C}$ is the central pressure. We can make the further assumption of a perfect gas and negligible radiation pressure, so that
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
	T_{C} \propto \frac{p_{C}\mu}{\rho} \propto \mu \frac{M}{R}
	}
	\label{eq:temperature_massradius}
\end{equation}
If we make the assumption of exclusively radiative transport, we find that 
\begin{equation*}
	\der{T}{r} \propto -\mu M R^{-2} \to L \propto -\frac{T^{3}}{\bar{\kappa}\rho}\der{T}{r}R^{2}
\end{equation*}
We then make the (very wrong, both conceptually and experimentally) approximation of constant opacity, thus finding
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
		L \propto \mu^{4}M^{3}
	}
	\label{eq:luminosity_mass}
\end{equation}
Finally, we can find a relation between luminosity and density
\begin{equation*}
	\der{L}{r} = 4\pi r^{2}\rho\epsilon \to \frac{L}{R^{3}} \propto \rho\epsilon \to \rho \propto \frac{L}{R^{3}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}
\end{equation*}
but we showed that $R \propto M/T$, so
\begin{equation*}
	\rho \propto \frac{T^{3}}{\epsilon}
\end{equation*}
without some kind of prescription for the energy production rate, this is as far as we can get. Fortunately for us, for main sequence stars that still burn Hydrogen, it is given (as we'll see) the following expression
\begin{equation*}
	\epsilon \propto \rho^{m}T^{n}
\end{equation*}
with $m=1$ both in the case of energy production through the $\text{ppI}$ chain and the CN-NO chain. The two of them, however, have really different dependencies on the temperature: The $\text{ppI}$ scales as $\epsilon \propto T^{4}$, while the CN-NO as $\epsilon\propto T^{15}$. Plugging in the aforementioned expression, we finally find
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
	\rho^{m+1} \propto T^{3-n}
	}
	\label{eq:density_temperature}
\end{equation}

\section{Politropic Structures}

A simple way of solving stellar structure equations is by killing off one of the variables, namely temperature. This isn't always possible, but there exist some special cases in which pressure can be assumed to be dependent only on density. This is the case of the so-called \emph{politropic structures}. Politropic structures are described by the following equation of state
\begin{equation}
	p(\rho) = K\rho(r)^{1+\frac{1}{n}} = K\rho(r)^{\Gamma}
	\label{eq:politropic}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the \emph{index of the politropic}. Consider the equation for hydrostatic equilibrium (\ref{eq:mechanical_eq}) and twist it so that
\begin{equation*}
	\der{p}{r}\frac{r^{2}}{\rho} = -GM_{r}
\end{equation*}
This we can derive with respect to the radial coordinate, finding
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}r}\left(\der{p}{r}\frac{r^{2}}{\rho}\right) = -G\der{M_{r}}{r} = -4\pi G \rho r^{2}
\end{equation*}
where we made use of (\ref{eq:massconservation}). Dividing by $r^{2}$ we finally obtain
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}r}\left(\der{p}{r}\frac{r^{2}}{\rho}\right) = -4\pi G \rho
	\label{eq:proto_le}
\end{equation}
which is still the same equation, but with a \emph{crispier} form that will make a little easier the discussion we'll do in the next few paragraphs.

\subsection*{Lane-Emden equation}
\par Assume for example a density profile of the form 
\begin{equation*}
    \rho(r) = \rho_{c}\phi^{n}(r)
\end{equation*}
with $\phi$ some adimensional density profile with the following characteristics: $\phi(0) = 1$ and $\partial_{r}\phi|_{r = 0} = 0$. Substituting in the politropic equation (\ref{eq:politropic}) we find that the pressure is simply
\begin{equation*}
	p(r) = K \rho_{c}^{1+\frac{1}{n}}\phi^{n+1} = p_{C}\phi^{n+1}
\end{equation*}
\par In terms of an adimensional radius $r = \lambda \xi$, with $\lambda$ constant depending only on $n$
\begin{equation*}
    \lambda = \left[\frac{K\rho_{c}^{1/n-1}(n+1)}{4\pi G}\right]^{1/2}
\end{equation*}
eq.\ref{eq:proto_le} takes a much simpler and elegant form
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
    \frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\partial_{\xi}(\xi^{2}\partial_{\xi}\phi) = -\phi^{n}
	}
    \label{eq:lane_emden}
\end{equation}
which is the general \emph{Lane-Emden equation}. Solutions for this equation have been calculated numerically in Fig.\ref{fig:LE}.
\par Note that solving (\ref{eq:lane_emden}) gives us two things: The profile density $\phi$ and the adimensional radius $\xi_{0}$ for which $\phi(\xi_{0}) = 0$, which is essentially the radius of the star.
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{img/lane_enden}
    \caption{Numerical solutions to the Lane-Emden equation.}
    \label{fig:LE}
\end{figure}
\par Since we know quite a few things already, we may ask ourselves if it is possible to calculate the central density of the star as well. Assuming that we know from some other observations the total mass of the star, we can indeed calculate $\rho_{c}$. In fact, we can simply integrate the equation of continuity (\ref{eq:massconservation}) up to the radius of the star
\begin{equation}
    M = \int_{0}^{R} 4\pi r^{2}\rho\,\text{d}r = 4\pi \lambda^{3}\rho_{c}\int_{0}^{\xi_{0}}\xi^{2}\phi^{n}\,\text{d}\xi
	\label{eq:politropic_mass}
\end{equation} 
\par The value of that integral is actually fixed by the original equation itself to $-\xi_{0}^{2}\partial_{\xi}\phi|_{\xi_{0}}$. So we can promptly invert the equation above and find the central density of the star. It goes without saying that once we've completely specified the density profile we'll have automatically determined the pressure profile as well by means of mere substitution.

\subsection*{Politropic Integration for the Solar Structure}

In the following section we'll try to integrate analitically the stellar structure equations for the Sun assuming a politropic EoS (\ref{eq:politropic}). Before we begin, let us make the following approximation
\begin{equation*}
	\beta = \frac{p_{\text{gas}}}{p_{\text{tot}}} \sim \text{const.}
\end{equation*}
where $p_{\text{tot}} = p_{\text{gas}}+p_{\text{rad}}$. This assumption follows from the conditions of hydrostatic equilibrium and radiative transfer, by means of which it's possible to prove that 
\begin{equation*}
	1-\beta \sim \bar{\kappa}(r)\epsilon
\end{equation*}
This is the so called \emph{Eddington Approximation}: Since $\epsilon \propto \rho T^{4}$ (at least as far as Hydrogen burning in main sequence stars is concerned) and $\bar{\kappa}\propto \rho^{\alpha}T^{-3.5}$ (Kramer's law), their product will be approximately constant\footnote{Note that this is only approximately true for Sun-like stars which are burning Hydrogen in the Main Sequence stage.}. 
\par Moreover
\begin{equation*}
	p_{\text{tot}} = p = p_{\text{gas}}+p_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\rho kT}{\mu m_{\text{H}}} + \frac{1}{3}aT^{4}
\end{equation*}
Let us use the expression for $\beta$ to remove the temperature, so to write a politropic equation
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{p_{\text{rad}}}{p_{\text{gas}}} = \frac{1-\beta}{\beta} = \frac{1}{3}\frac{aT^{3}}{\rho}\mu m_{\text{H}}
\end{equation*}
so that
\begin{equation*}
	T = \left[\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\frac{3\rho}{\mu m_{\text{H}}a}\right]^{1/3}
\end{equation*}
\par This way, if we substitute in the equation for the pressure, we find
\begin{equation}
	p = \left[\frac{3}{a}\left(\frac{k}{\mu m_{\text{H}}}\right)^{4}\frac{1-\beta}{\beta^{4}}\right]^{1/3}\rho^{4/3}
	\label{eq:solar_politropicequation}
\end{equation}
which is a politropic equation as requested. With reference to (\ref{eq:politropic}), this relation would have a politropic index $n=3$. Note that in this case, since we don't know $\beta$, we don't know the multiplicative factor, nor we have the absolute certainty of it being constant. For the moment, we'll consider an averaged molecular weight over the whole stellar structure\footnote{Note that generally the solar structure is divided into two regions, one where Hydrogen burning has happened (\emph{solar interior}: $X \sim 0.34$, $Y\sim 0.64$, $Z \sim 0.02$ and $\mu \approx 0.85$, assuming complete ionization) and one where it has not (\emph{external envelope}: $X \sim 0.70$, $Y\sim 0.28$, $Z \sim 0.02$ and $\mu \approx 0.62$, assuming complete ionization).}.
\par We still don't know the multiplicative constant, but we know both the mass and the radius of the Sun. We've shown in (\ref{eq:politropic_mass}) that if we know both the mass and the radius of a star we're able to infer its central density
\begin{equation*}
	 M = M(\beta, \rho_{C})= 4\pi \lambda^{3}\rho_{c}(-\xi^{2}\partial_{\xi}\phi)_{\xi_{0}} = M_{\odot}
\end{equation*}
with 
\begin{equation*}
    \lambda = \left[\frac{K\rho_{c}^{1/n-1}(n+1)}{4\pi G}\right]^{1/2}
\end{equation*}
Moreover, we know that $R_{\odot} = R(\beta, \rho_{C}) \lambda\xi_{0}$, thus we have enough equations to solve for. Behold
\begin{equation*}
	\rho_{C} = \frac{M_{\odot}}{4\pi R_{\odot}^{3}}\frac{\xi_{0}^{3}}{(-\xi^{2}\partial_{\xi}\phi)_{\xi_{0}}}
\end{equation*}
So if we plug in the necessary solar parameters and look up to some table to find the values for $\xi_{0} \approx 6.9$ and $-\xi^{2}\partial_{\xi}\phi|_{\xi_{0}} \approx 2.02$ for a politropic index $n=3$, we'd find
\begin{equation*}
	\rho_{C} \sim 75\,\text{g}\,\text{cm}^{-3}
\end{equation*}
which is about half of the best estimate we'd find using less approximate models, so everything considered we've done a good job.
\par At this point we can equate the expression for $K$ coming from the definition of the parameter $\lambda$ and that coming from the multiplicative factor of (\ref{eq:solar_politropicequation})
\begin{equation*}
	K = \frac{\lambda^{2} 4 \pi G}{(1+n)\rho_{c}^{1/n-1}} \quad K_{\odot} = \left[\frac{3}{a}\left(\frac{k}{\mu m_{\text{H}}}\right)^{4}\frac{1-\beta}{\beta^{4}}\right]^{1/3}
\end{equation*}
thus allowing us to solve for $\beta$ (since we've calculated $\rho_{C}$ and we can deduce the value of $\lambda = R_{\odot}/\xi_{0}$). The solution is not exactly trivial, and is shown in (\ref{eq:betafactor})
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1-\beta}{\mu \beta^{4}} = \frac{a\pi}{48}\frac{G^{3}M_{\odot}^{2}m_{\text{H}^{4}}}{k^{4}(-\xi^{2}\partial_{\xi}\phi)_{\xi_{0}}} = A
	\label{eq:betafactor}
\end{equation}
which you can use also for sun-like stars. Given what we've found earlier we can substitute for $\beta \approx 0.998$, meaning that most of the pressure is due to raw gas pressure (and not from radiation); the value of $\beta$ coming from evolution models is $\beta\sim0.999$ which is in great agreement with our present approximated result.
\par We can also calculate the central pressure $p_{C}\sim 1.2\cdot 10^{17}\,\text{dyne}/\text{cm}^{2}$ and the central temperature $T_{C}\sim 1.6\cdot 10^{7}\,\text{K}$.
\par Similarly to what we've done in (\ref{eq:politropic_mass}), we can calculate the mass within a sphere of radius $r$. The expression is almost identical
\begin{equation}
    M = \int_{0}^{r} 4\pi r^{2}\rho\,\text{d}r = 4\pi \lambda^{3}\rho_{c}\int_{0}^{\xi}\xi^{2}\phi^{n}\,\text{d}\xi = 4\pi \lambda^{3}\rho_{c}(-\xi^{2}\partial_{\xi}\phi)
\end{equation} 

\section{Homologous Models: Self-Similarity}

\par Let's start with a simple consideration: Different politropic structures, \emph{with the same politropic index $n$}, differ only for a scaling factor. This is not particularly difficult to show. Consider
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\rho(\xi)}{\rho'(\xi)} = \frac{\rho_{C}}{\rho_{C}'}
\end{equation*}
and the same thing holds for the pressure, mass and so on
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{m(\xi)}{m'(\xi)} = \frac{M}{M'} \quad \frac{r(\xi)}{r'(\xi)} = \frac{R}{R'}
\end{equation*}
\par All we have done was writing down the various politropic equations (and what descends from them) and \emph{scale} them, finding out that you can jump from one another by simply rescaling an already existent solution. That is a notable simplification if you think about it.
\par What are, then, in general the properties a star must have to be self-similar? The answer you can find in the following Table.
\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced,attach boxed title to top center={yshift=-3mm,yshifttext=-1mm},
colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!75!black,colbacktitle=blue!80!black,
title=Self-Similarity,
boxed title style={size=small,colframe=blue!50!black}]
Suppose to have a quantity dependent on two independent variables $Q(x,y)$. Characteristic scales $Q_{0}(x)$ and $\delta(x)$ are defined for $Q,\,y$ respectively. Then the scaled variables are just 
	\begin{equation*}
		\xi = \frac{y}{\delta(x)} \quad \tilde{Q}(\xi, x)=\frac{Q(x,\xi)}{Q_{0}(x)}
	\end{equation*}
	If the scaled dependent variable is independent of $x$, i.e.
	\begin{equation*}
		\exists\hat{Q}(\xi): \tilde{Q}(\xi, x) = \hat{Q}(\xi)
	\end{equation*}
	then $Q(x,y)$ is self-similar. The interested reader can refer, for example, to \cite{Pope_2000}.
\end{tcolorbox}

\par Let's make a few other definitions, starting from $\eta = m(r)/M(R)$
\begin{align*}
	\frac{r(\eta)}{r'(\eta)} = z \quad & \quad \frac{P(\eta)}{P'(\eta)} = p \\
	\frac{T(\eta)}{T'(\eta)} = t \quad & \quad \frac{L(\eta)}{L'(\eta)} = s \\
	\frac{\rho(\eta)}{\rho'(\eta)} = d \quad & \quad
\end{align*}
Given these definitions, we'll be interested in finding out how these scaling factors depend on the variations in mass and chemical composition. We'll then define
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{M(\eta)}{M'(\eta)} = x \quad \frac{\mu(\eta)}{\mu'(\eta)} = y
\end{equation*}
Tragically, we have to make another approximation: We have to assume that also the opacity and the energy production rate are self-similar
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\epsilon(\eta)}{\epsilon'(\eta)} = \varepsilon \quad \frac{\bar{\kappa}(\eta)}{\bar{\kappa}'(\eta)} = k
\end{equation*}
\par This is a very restrictive limitation to the possible self-similar stellar structures: We've already seen that for the energy production rate the greater the mass, the greater the temperature and the greater gets the energy production rate, so we can't really compare much different masses. A similar thing holds for the opacity. This works kinda well for stars that have just entered the main sequence, when nuclear reactions haven't really started.
\par We can write all the structure equations in a self-similar fashion
\begin{equation*}
	\der{p}{\eta} = \der{p}{r}\der{r}{\eta} = \der{p}{r}\frac{M}{4\pi\rho r^{2}}
\end{equation*}
so that plugging this in the hydrostatic equilibrium condition yields
\begin{equation*}
	\der{p}{\eta} = -\frac{G}{4\pi}\frac{M^{2}\eta}{r^{4}}
\end{equation*}
for both the structures we're considering (\emph{mutatis mutandis}, that is). We can extend this calculation to all the other equations and start using all the self-similar relations we've written above. This way we can write
\begin{align*}
	\der{p'}{\eta} &= -\frac{G}{4\pi}\frac{M'^{2}\eta}{r'^{4}}\left(\frac{x^{2}}{z^{4}p}\right) \\
	\der{r'}{\eta} &= \frac{M'}{4\pi\rho'r'^{2}}\left(\frac{x}{z^{3}d}\right)\\
	\der{T'}{\eta} &= -\frac{3}{64a\pi^{2}c}\frac{\bar{\kappa}'M'L'(\eta)}{r'^{4}T'^{3}}\left(\frac{kxs}{z^{4}t^{4}}\right)\\
	\der{L'}{\eta} &= M'\epsilon'\left(\frac{\varepsilon x}{s}\right)
\end{align*}
but since the stellar structure equations must hold also for the second star (the one with the primed superscript), all the terms within parentheses must be equal to 1!
\begin{align}
	1 = \frac{x^{2}}{z^{4}p} \quad & \quad 1 = \frac{x}{z^{3}d}\\
	1 = \frac{kxs}{z^{4}t^{4}} \quad & \quad 1 = \frac{\varepsilon x}{s}
\end{align}
and we also have the equation of state, so we have enough equations to solve this\footnote{Note that the EoS isn't enough to completely specify and solve the system. We have seven variables and only four equations, meaning we're in the need of three other relations. These can be found in the EoS $\rho \propto p^{\alpha}T^{-\delta}\mu^{-\phi}$, the energy production rate $\epsilon \propto \rho^{\lambda}T^{\nu}$ and the opacity $\bar{\kappa}\propto p^{a}T^{b}$.}. It is possible to show that the system of equations can be solved with solutions given by
\begin{align*}
	R:\;z = x^{z_{1}}y^{z_{2}} \quad & \quad L:\;s = x^{s_{1}}y^{s_{2}}\\
	P:\;p = x^{p_{1}}y^{p_{2}} \quad & \quad T:\;t = x^{t_{1}}y^{t_{2}}
\end{align*}
thus reducing the equations to equations for the powers of $x$ and $y$, whose sum must necessary be equal to zero (by simple means of dimensional analysis). You can read an example of solution right below (or in any book that deals with self-similar solutions for stellar structures)
\begin{equation*}
	z_{1} = \frac{1}{2}(1+A) \quad A = \left[\frac{4\delta(1+a+\lambda\alpha)}{\nu+b-4-\lambda\delta}+4\alpha-3\right]^{-1}
\end{equation*}
